Welcome to Microsoft Copilot Training for Business, from Black Wealth Exchange. I'm Marcus, and over the next twelve lessons I'm going to help you use Microsoft Copilot to get real time back in your week, without putting your customers or your reputation at risk.

Let's start with what AI actually is, because once you understand that, everything else in this course makes sense.

Tools like Copilot work by predicting useful words. They've learned patterns from an enormous amount of text, so they're fast at drafting, summarizing, reorganizing and explaining. Ask for a polite reply to a customer and you'll have one in seconds.

But here's the part people skip. Predicting likely words is not the same as knowing the truth. Copilot doesn't know your prices. It doesn't know your return policy. It doesn't know that Mrs. Johnson has been a customer for eleven years. Unless you tell it, it will guess, and its guesses can sound very confident. So the first rule of this course is simple: AI writes the draft, and you check it.

Now, where should you use it? The business owners I see get the most from AI don't start with the tool. They start with a task. Something that happens often, takes real time, and follows a pattern. Answering the same pricing questions. Writing follow-up emails. Turning meeting notes into a to-do list. Building a weekly report from messy numbers.

Before you change anything, I want you to write down how that task works today. How many times a week does it happen? How many minutes does it take? What mistakes happen? And who has to approve the result? That's your baseline. Without a baseline, you can't tell whether AI actually saved you time, or just felt faster.

Then rate the risk. A typo in an internal note is a small problem. A wrong price in a customer quote, or a wrong statement in anything legal or financial, is a big one. Your first AI project should be low risk, easy to check, and done often enough that the minutes add up.

Let me show you what that looks like. Picture a small catering company. The owner lists six weekly tasks: answering pricing questions, confirming bookings, writing social posts, ordering supplies, sending invoices, and replying to reviews.

Next to each one, she writes how often it happens and how long it takes. Pricing questions come in about fifteen times a week, six minutes each. That's ninety minutes, every single week.

Then she marks the risk. Pricing replies are medium risk, because a wrong number would confuse a customer. But she reads every reply before it goes out, so a mistake would be caught.

So her best first pilot is drafting replies to pricing questions. Frequent, repetitive, and checked by a person before it reaches anyone. That's exactly the kind of task we want.

Your assignment for this lesson is to build the same table for your business. List at least six recurring tasks. For each one, write how often it happens, the minutes it takes, common mistakes, the risk level and who approves it. Then circle one low-risk task as your pilot, and write one sentence about why you chose it.

Hold on to that table. We'll build on it through the whole course, and in the final lesson it becomes the starting point for your thirty-day plan.
