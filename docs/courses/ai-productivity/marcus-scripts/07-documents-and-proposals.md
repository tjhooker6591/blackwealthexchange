# Lesson 7 — Documents and Proposals
Presenter: Marcus · Target length: about 3–4 minutes · Paste everything below the line into HeyGen.

---

Writing a proposal from a blank page can eat a whole afternoon. Copilot is genuinely good at this kind of work: turning your notes into headings, filling in the standard sections, and making the writing clear. If you have Microsoft 365 Copilot, you can do this right inside Word. If not, draft it in Copilot and paste it into your document.

A strong small-business proposal answers a handful of simple questions. What problem does the client have? What will you do about it? What will you deliver, and when? What's not included? What do you need from the client? What does it cost? And what happens next?

Write your answers to those questions, give them to Copilot, and you'll get a clean first draft in a minute or two.

But I need to warn you about something. If you ask for a persuasive proposal, Copilot may try to persuade by making things up. You'll see phrases like "proven to increase revenue by thirty percent," or a glowing quote from "a satisfied client." If you can't prove it, it can't go in. Invented results and fake testimonials don't just embarrass you; they can break advertising rules and cost you a client's trust.

So, two habits. First, any price you haven't set should say TBD. Second, before you send anything, build a quick fact-check table: every claim in the document, where the evidence is, and whether it's approved. It takes five minutes and it protects your name.

Let's practice. Imagine a made-up client: a local auto-repair shop that loses business when customers forget their appointments. You're offering to set up text reminders.

Run the prompt on the lesson page. It asks for a one-page outline with these headings: goal, what we'll deliver, not included, what we need from you, timeline, price, risks, and next step. It labels every price TBD and asks for no statistics, guarantees or testimonials.

Now read the draft, looking for any number, result or promise. Even with those instructions, you might find something like "reduces no-shows by forty percent." Unless you have proof, delete it, or rewrite it as a goal: "Our goal is to reduce missed appointments."

Then make your fact-check table with three columns: claim, evidence, approved. Only send the proposal when every row says yes.

You'll notice the "not included" section is often the most valuable part. It's where you prevent the misunderstandings that turn into unpaid work later.

Your assignment: pick a service you offer, or could offer, and a made-up client. Use Copilot to draft a one-page proposal with the headings from the walkthrough. Build a fact-check table for every claim, and remove or fix anything you can't support.
