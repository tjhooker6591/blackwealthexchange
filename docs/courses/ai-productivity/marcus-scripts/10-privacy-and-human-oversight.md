# Lesson 10 — Privacy, Security and Human Oversight
Presenter: Marcus · Target length: about 3–4 minutes · Paste everything below the line into HeyGen.

---

Everything you paste into an AI tool goes somewhere. So before you use Copilot for real work, I want you to sort your information into four groups.

Public. That's your website, your menu, your posted prices. Internal. Schedules, process notes, how you do things. Confidential. Customer lists, contracts, pricing strategy, employee details. And regulated. Payment card numbers, Social Security numbers, health information, anything the law specifically protects.

Public and most internal information is usually fine in an approved tool. Confidential information should only go into a tool your business has approved for it, such as Microsoft 365 Copilot under a work account, and only if your settings and policies allow it. Regulated information and passwords should never be pasted into a general AI chat. Not even once, not even to save time.

When you practice, or when you test a new prompt, replace the real details with placeholders. Customer becomes CUSTOMER in brackets. The phone number becomes PHONE. The account number becomes REMOVED. Then read it again to make sure nothing that identifies a real person is left before you press enter.

Let me show you. Here's a made-up complaint: "Hi, this is Dana Lewis, four-oh-four, five-five-five, oh-one-eight-eight, card ending forty-four twenty-one. My order seventy-seven eighty-one never came."

Replace the details: "Hi, this is CUSTOMER, PHONE, card ending REMOVED. My order ORDER NUMBER never came." Check again: is there an email, an address, anything else? Remove it.

Now ask Copilot for a kind reply, using the prompt on the lesson page. No refund promise, no date, and it ends by saying you're checking with the carrier and will update them within one business day. When you send it from your own email, you put the real name back in.

Notice something: Copilot didn't need the card number or the phone number to write a good reply. It almost never does.

The second half of this lesson is human oversight. That means a person stays in charge. Decide which AI outputs need approval before they're used, who approves them, and what happens when AI gets something wrong. Watch for unfair or biased wording, especially in anything about hiring, pricing or customers. And treat every AI result as a draft until a person signs off.

Your assignment is to write a one-page AI use policy for your business. Include the information that's allowed, the information that's never allowed, the approved tools and accounts, which outputs need approval and by whom, how to report a mistake, and who owns the policy. Then practice redacting one made-up customer message.

It might feel like paperwork. It's actually what lets you use AI with confidence, and lets your team use it without you looking over their shoulder.
