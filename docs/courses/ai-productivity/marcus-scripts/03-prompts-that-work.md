# Lesson 3 — Prompts That Work: The Four-Part Prompt
Presenter: Marcus · Target length: about 3–4 minutes · Paste everything below the line into HeyGen.

---

If you take one skill away from this course, make it this one. The quality of what Copilot gives you depends almost entirely on what you ask for.

A prompt is just the instruction you type. Short, vague prompts get generic answers. If you type "write an email to a customer," Copilot has to guess who the customer is, what happened and what you want. It'll guess. And it might guess a refund you never offered.

So I want you to use four parts every time. Role, context, task, and constraints.

Role is who Copilot should act as. "You are the front-desk manager of a family dental office."

Context is the facts it needs. "The patient has missed two appointments. Our policy is a twenty-five dollar fee after the second."

Task is exactly what you want. "Write a friendly text, under a hundred words, reminding them of the policy."

Constraints are the rules. "Don't threaten. Don't mention any fee other than twenty-five dollars. Keep it warm."

You can add a fifth part, format, if you want the answer as a table, a checklist, or an email with a subject line.

Now, the most important constraint of all is this: don't invent facts. Tell Copilot what to do when something is missing. I like this line: "If a price, date or name isn't given, write CONFIRM in brackets instead of guessing." That one sentence prevents most of the AI mistakes I see in business writing.

Let's see the difference.

First, the vague version. Type: "Write an email about a late order." Look at what comes back. It'll make up a product. It might make up a delivery date. It might offer a discount. None of that came from you.

Now the four-part version, using the sample on the lesson page. Role: you're the customer care lead for a small online candle shop. Context: order ten forty-two, three candles, was due Friday, the supplier is late, and we expect to ship within five business days, but that's not confirmed. Task: write a short apology email with a subject line and one clear next step. Constraints: under a hundred and twenty words, warm and honest, don't promise a delivery date or a discount, and write CONFIRM for anything unknown.

Compare the two. Which one stated facts you never gave it? Which one could you send after a quick read? Time yourself editing each one. That difference is the time you're getting back.

And when a draft isn't quite right, don't start over. Just reply. "Shorter." "Less formal." "Take out the second paragraph." Copilot remembers the conversation, so small corrections are fast.

When you land on a prompt that works, save it. I call that a prompt card: the role, the context fields you fill in each time, the task and the constraints. You and your team can reuse it, and you'll get consistent results every time.

Your assignment: pick a writing task from your Lesson 1 table. Write a vague prompt and a four-part prompt for it, using made-up details. Run both. Score each from one to five on accuracy, tone, and how long it took to edit. Then save the better one as your first prompt card, with blanks for the details that change.
